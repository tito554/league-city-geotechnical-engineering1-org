We ran a series of Lugeon tests last fall for a detention basin expansion off West Walker Street, right where the old floodplain deposits transition into the Beaumont Formation clays. The contractor had assumed uniform permeability across the site based on a single lab test, but we were seeing 10 to 50 Lugeon units in the upper 15 feet of silty sand while the underlying stiff clay barely took 2 units. That kind of vertical contrast is exactly why a field permeability test in League City isn't optional when groundwater control makes or breaks the earthwork schedule. In-situ methods like the Lefranc test for soil and the Lugeon test for rock intervals give you hydraulic conductivity values tied to actual stratigraphy, not remolded lab specimens. For any project within the Clear Creek watershed or near the Dickinson Bayou drainage basin, where seasonal perched water and relic channel sands create unpredictable flow paths, combining field permeability data with a CPT soil profile across the footprint lets the design team size dewatering systems and cutoff walls with real numbers instead of textbook ranges.

A single Lugeon test across a fractured clay layer tells you more about real seepage potential than a dozen lab permeameter runs on intact specimens.

Process overview

League City sits barely 20 feet above sea level on average, with a subtropical climate that dumps over 50 inches of rain annually and a shallow groundwater table that fluctuates seasonally between 4 and 12 feet below grade. This hydrogeologic setting makes the Lefranc test – essentially a variable-head or constant-head injection test in a borehole isolated by packers – the workhorse for measuring hydraulic conductivity in soils at the depths where most spread footings and utilities are placed. We typically run Lefranc tests in 5-foot increments through the Beaumont Formation clays and interbedded silts using a 2-inch diameter slotted screen with clean sand pack isolation. When the investigation hits the underlying sand layers that serve as the primary aquifer recharge zone, the Lugeon test becomes essential: we isolate the test interval with pneumatic packers, inject water in five pressure stages following the Houlsby method, and interpret the flow-versus-pressure curve to distinguish laminar flow from dilation, wash-out, or void filling. The resulting Lugeon values – typically between 3 and 30 units for the Pleistocene-age sands encountered in Galveston County – feed directly into seepage analyses and grouting designs. For sites where the stratigraphy is particularly heterogeneous, integrating field permeability data with grain size distribution curves from split-spoon samples helps validate whether the in-situ K-value aligns with Hazen or Kozeny-Carman estimates or whether macropores and preferential flow paths are dominating the response.

Local ground factors

The field setup we mobilize for permeability testing in League City involves a truck-mounted drill rig, twin pneumatic packers with a water swivel assembly, a calibrated flowmeter and pressure transducer array, and a data logger that records flow and pressure at one-second intervals throughout each pressure stage. The equipment is straightforward, but the real risk sits in how the data gets used – or misused. A Lefranc test run without proper filter cake removal or a Lugeon stage where the packer loses seal against a weathered claystone borehole wall will produce K-values that look plausible on a log but underestimate true mass permeability by an order of magnitude. We've reviewed reports from sites near the Gulf Freeway where designers accepted a single-stage slug test result as representative of an entire excavation depth, only to hit flowing sands at 18 feet that flooded the footing excavations and delayed the project six weeks. In League City's layered coastal plain deposits, the standard of care demands at least three test intervals per borehole, with packer seating verified by pressure-decay checks between stages. When the investigation is tied to a deep excavation monitoring plan, the permeability profile directly informs the dewatering well spacing and pump capacity needed to maintain a dry working platform through the wet season.

Related services

01

Lefranc Variable-Head and Constant-Head Testing

For assessing soil permeability, we carry out borehole tests employing slotted casings isolated with sand packs. Depending on the anticipated hydraulic conductivity range, either falling-head or constant-head procedures are applied, monitored via real-time transducers. These tests are suited for designing dewatering systems, evaluating infiltration basin viability, and confirming permeability in earthen embankments located within the Clear Creek floodplain.

02

Lugeon Packer Testing in Rock and Indurated Layers

Utilizing either single or double pneumatic packers, we perform multi-stage pressure injection tests in line with the Houlsby method. Each test interval includes five pressure stages, with flow versus pressure analysis to define fracture flow conditions. These tests are used for dam foundation evaluations, verifying grouting designs, and profiling permeability in deep shafts where the Beaumont Formation grades into cemented sand strata.

03

Integrated Hydrogeologic Characterization

We integrate field permeability measurements with piezometer placement, slug tests, and lab-based permeability tests on undisturbed samples. In-situ hydraulic conductivity values are correlated against CPT soil behavior types and grain size distributions to develop a comprehensive site hydraulic conductivity model, applicable for MODFLOW input or SEEP/W seepage analyses.

Reference standards

The referenced standards include ASTM D4630-19, which covers in situ constant head injection tests for determining transmissivity and storage coefficient in low-permeability rocks; USBR 7300-89, detailing the procedure for Lugeon tests in rock boreholes by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation; and ISO 22282-2:2012, which addresses geohydraulic testing for water permeability in boreholes using open systems.

Quick answers

What is the cost range for field permeability testing in League City?

In a standard investigation that includes two or three Lefranc test intervals or one Lugeon profile with several pressure stages, the typical project cost in League City falls between US$600 and US$1,010. This range varies based on mobilization distance, borehole depth, and the total number of required test intervals. The final work plan is always customized to fit the site’s stratigraphy and the specific hydraulic queries from the design team.

When should I specify a Lugeon test instead of a Lefranc test?

The selection depends on the tested material. Lefranc tests are effective in soils—such as clays, silts, and sands—where a slotted screen can be installed in an open borehole. Lugeon tests target rock or heavily cemented layers requiring pneumatic packers to isolate specific intervals and apply staged pressures. In League City, we typically employ Lefranc testing on the upper Beaumont Formation clays and transition to Lugeon when the borehole hits cemented sand or calcareous sandstone layers, which are encountered at depths of 25 to 40 feet in certain locations.

How many test intervals do you recommend per borehole?

During site investigations in the coastal plain deposits near League City, we conduct at least three test intervals per borehole, spaced to reflect changes in stratigraphy. For instance, if the top 10 feet consist of fissured desiccated clay, the following 15 feet are hard Beaumont clay, and the underlying interval intersects a water-bearing sand lens—each zone may exhibit hydraulic conductivity values differing by up to two orders of magnitude. Testing just one zone and then extrapolating is the most frequent error we observe in unsuccessful dewatering designs.

How do you interpret Lugeon test results for fracture flow versus matrix permeability?

For each of the five stages, we graph flow rate versus effective pressure and examine the curve’s shape. A linear trend passing through the origin signifies laminar flow through the rock matrix. Hysteresis loops—where the pressure reduction curve lies above the increase curve—indicate fracture dilation or washout. A curve that plateaus at elevated pressures suggests fracture infilling or turbulent flow. This analysis adheres to Houlsby’s 1976 classification, and results are recorded per test interval in the report, enabling the design engineer to incorporate suitable reduction factors for seepage analysis.

What are the typical hydraulic conductivity values for League City soils?

For the Beaumont Formation clays beneath most of League City, Lefranc tests typically yield hydraulic conductivity ranging from 1×10⁻⁶ to 5×10⁻⁴ cm/s, with the upper values associated with siltier or sandier interbeds. Deeper Pleistocene sands exhibit K-values between 1×10⁻⁴ and 1×10⁻² cm/s, making dewatering well spacing crucial when excavations go deeper than 15 to 20 feet. These measurements align with published data for Gulf Coast Pleistocene terrace deposits.
