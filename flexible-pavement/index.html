Flexible Pavement Design in League City, TX: Meeting AASHTO 93 on Gulf Coast Soils

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

LEARN MORE →

AASHTO 93 remains the backbone of flexible pavement design across Texas, but applying it in League City demands more than just plugging numbers into an equation. The city sits at roughly 20 feet above sea level, on soils that transition from Beaumont Formation clays to silty deposits near Clear Creek. This means the structural number alone is worthless without a reliable resilient modulus backed by local subgrade data. Our approach starts with site-specific CBR testing for roads to anchor the design inputs, then builds the layer coefficients around the real drainage conditions that define performance in this part of Galveston County.

League City's high water table and expansive clays mean a pavement layer coefficient is only as good as the drainage factor behind it.

Process overview

A recent rehabilitation project off FM 518 illustrated the challenge clearly. The existing section had rutting exceeding half an inch after only five years, yet the original design met standard TxDOT specifications. Cores revealed the problem was not the asphalt thickness but the base course, which had lost permeability because of fines migrating from the clay subgrade during seasonal saturation. We redesigned the section by separating the base from the subgrade with a geotextile and increasing the HMA surface to 3 inches. The granular base was specified at 8 inches of flex base, compacted to 98 percent of modified Proctor density, with values verified through our Proctor and density testing. The structural number was recalculated using the resilient modulus derived from triaxial testing, and the drainage coefficient was adjusted to reflect the actual time the base spends near saturation. In League City, where the water table can rise to within 2 feet of the surface after heavy rain, that drainage coefficient often makes or breaks the pavement section. Complementary sand cone density testing during construction ensured that field compaction matched the laboratory target.

Local ground factors

The distinctive distress pattern for asphalt pavements in League City arises from the local geography: the subgrade undergoes prolonged saturation during hurricane season, then intense drying that induces shrinkage cracks in the clay. In many neighborhoods near Clear Lake, the Plasticity Index exceeds 25, and the resulting shrink-swell cycle pumps fines into the granular base, gradually lowering the effective structural number. This leads to alligator cracking well before the design life is reached, especially when the pavement also acts as a flood barrier during storm surges—making underdesign a problem beyond just maintenance costs. Our team uses Atterberg limits correlated with the Thornthwaite Moisture Index for the Houston-Galveston area to assign a realistic equilibrium suction, which directly informs the resilient modulus estimation within the AASHTO equation.

Related services

01

Subgrade Characterization and Mr Testing

For expansive clays, we conduct laboratory resilient modulus evaluation via cyclic triaxial loading, complemented by CBR correlation and suction-controlled tests. Specimens are prepared at target moisture contents that represent post-construction equilibrium conditions, rather than simply the optimum moisture content.

02

Structural Section Design per AASHTO 93

We completely determine the structural number (SN), layer thicknesses, and material requirements for the HMA surface, binder course, flex base, and subbase. Drainage coefficients are assigned using site-specific groundwater data and the time-to-drain criterion.

03

Construction QA/QC Testing

On-site density confirmation is performed using nuclear density gauge and sand cone techniques, along with asphalt content extraction and gradation checks on base materials. Real-time compaction tracking helps prevent the post-construction settlement that commonly affects League City subdivisions.

Reference standards

The design references the AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures (1993, with 1998 supplement). Compaction testing follows ASTM D1557 (Modified Proctor), CBR testing per ASTM D1883 / AASHTO T 193, resilient modulus per AASHTO T 307, and Atterberg limits for expansive soil classification per ASTM D4318.

Quick answers

What typical pavement section does League City require for a residential street?

In League City, most residential streets are designed with 2 to 2.5 inches of Type D HMA over an 8-inch flex base, assuming a subgrade CBR of at least 5%. When the Plasticity Index exceeds 20, we recommend increasing the base thickness to 10 inches and adding a geotextile separator. The exact structural number is based on the 20-year ESAL projection, which for low-volume residential roads typically falls between 50,000 and 150,000.

How much does a flexible pavement design package cost?

For a typical League City project, the cost of the design package—including subgrade investigation, laboratory testing, and pavement structural calculations—ranges from US$1,760 to US$5,000, depending on the number of borings and the complexity of the drainage analysis.

How do you account for the expansive clay subgrades common in Galveston County?

From each boring, we measure the Atterberg limits and the percentage passing the #200 sieve. Using the Thornthwaite Moisture Index for the Houston-Galveston region, we estimate the equilibrium suction in the subgrade. This suction is then converted into an effective resilient modulus with the Enhanced Integrated Climatic Model (EICM), which provides a much more realistic input for the AASHTO 93 equation than a simple CBR correlation at optimum moisture.
