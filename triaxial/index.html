Triaxial Testing in League City: Shear Strength for Coastal Plain Soils

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

LEARN MORE →

League City's development accelerated after the 1960s NASA expansion, transforming marshlands and prairie into subdivisions. The local geology, dominated by Pleistocene Beaumont Formation clays and silts with interbedded sand lenses, presents a specific challenge: these overconsolidated clays lose strength when saturated, a critical factor given the city's flat topography and slow surface drainage toward Clear Creek. For any project involving embankments, deep excavations, or heavily loaded shallow footings near the 29.5° N latitude, determining the effective shear strength is not a formality. A triaxial test provides the drained and undrained parameters—cohesion and friction angle—that direct simple shear or unconfined compression simply cannot resolve for multi-stage loading scenarios.

An effective friction angle derived from CU triaxial testing on Beaumont clay typically runs 5° to 7° lower than peak total stress values—ignoring that gap is what triggers long-term slope failures in Clear Creek watershed developments.

Process overview

On sites west of I-45 where the gumbo clay runs deep, we consistently observe that remolded strength is only a fraction of the peak value. This sensitivity means that a standard penetration test alone won't predict how the soil behaves under deviatoric stress during a hurricane-season saturation cycle. A consolidated-undrained triaxial test with pore pressure measurement, run per ASTM D4767, gives us the effective stress friction angle—often in the 22° to 28° range for intact Beaumont clay. The procedure involves trimming an undisturbed Shelby tube specimen into a 2.8-inch diameter cylinder, saturating it under back pressure, and shearing it at a controlled strain rate. The resulting Mohr-Coulomb envelope then feeds directly into slope stability models or retaining wall designs. For granular layers encountered below 15 feet, we switch to a drained test per ASTM D7181 to capture the dilatant behavior of the sand without generating excess pore pressure.

Local ground factors

With an elevation of barely 20 feet above sea level in League City, the subtropical humidity keeps the active clay zone near full saturation for extended periods. This is far from a dry-clay environment. If a contractor excavates for a detention pond or stormwater lift station without understanding the effective stress envelope, temporary slopes may stand vertical for a week but can collapse overnight following a summer downpour. The laminated fabric of the Beaumont clay exacerbates the risk: slickensides and silt partings create weak planes that bulk samples miss. Conducting three-stage triaxial compression on oriented specimens reveals this anisotropy. Relying on published correlations from the Dallas chalk or Edwards Plateau misinterprets the local Pleistocene depositional history, leading to retaining structures that are either dangerously under-designed or unnecessarily massive.

Related services

01

Consolidated Undrained (CU) with Pore Pressure

The process involves three-stage isotropic consolidation followed by undrained shear at a rate slow enough for pore pressure equalization. The effective stress path and Mohr circles are then reported.

02

Unconsolidated Undrained (UU) Quick Check

For total stress cohesion in fully saturated clays, ASTM D2850 is followed. This procedure is used during preliminary grading to verify bearing capacity assumptions before full CU results become available.

03

Consolidated Drained (CD) for Granular Layers

Open drainage shear is performed at a rate of 0.005 in/min or slower to prevent pore pressure buildup. This test determines the critical state friction angle for sandy interbeds located beneath the clay crust.

04

Stress Path and Modulus Reporting

Post-peak strength, we calculate the secant modulus at 50% failure (E_50) and Skempton's pore pressure coefficient at failure (Af) for use in FEM models of embankment settlement.

Reference standards

The relevant standards are: ASTM D4767-11 (Consolidated Undrained Triaxial Compression Test for Cohesive Soils), ASTM D2850-15 (Unconsolidated Undrained Triaxial Compression Test), ASTM D7181-20 (Consolidated Drained Triaxial Compression Test for Soils), AASHTO T-297 (Consolidated Undrained Triaxial Test), and USACE EM 1110-2-1906 (Laboratory Soils Testing).

Quick answers

How much does a triaxial test program cost for a League City building site?

A standard set of three CU triaxial tests on undisturbed Shelby tube samples ranges from US$2,150 to US$3,020, depending on the number of confining stages and whether modulus data is needed. A single UU test falls at the lower end of that range.

What's the difference between the triaxial test and the unconfined compression test?

The unconfined test shears the sample with zero lateral pressure—it is fast but provides only undrained shear strength for saturated, intact clay. In contrast, the triaxial test applies controlled confining pressure, measuring both drained and undrained strength at realistic in-situ stress levels and yielding the friction angle that the unconfined test cannot.

How long does it take to get triaxial test results?

A CU test with three effective confining stresses typically takes seven to ten business days from sample extrusion to final report. Saturation alone can require 48 hours for stiff Beaumont clay. We can expedite to five business days when the project schedule demands, but the consolidation phase cannot be shortened without compromising effective stress data.

Do you test granular soils in the triaxial cell for League City projects?

Yes. When borings encounter sand layers beneath the Beaumont clay—common along the Clear Creek alluvial corridor—we perform consolidated drained (CD) tests on reconstituted specimens compacted to field density. This captures the dilation angle and critical state strength that govern pile end-bearing capacity in the sand stratum.
