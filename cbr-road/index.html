Laboratory CBR Testing in League City: Subgrade Strength for Pavement Design

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

LEARN MORE →

The soil profile between Clear Creek and Dickinson Bayou can look dramatically different within a half-mile in League City. One site near the 518 corridor might hit sandy loam at two feet, while another farther south toward the floodplain finds fat clay extending past eight feet. For pavement engineers, this variability means a single assumed CBR value is never safe. Laboratory California Bearing Ratio testing under ASTM D1883 gives us a controlled, repeatable measurement of subgrade strength, independent of field moisture fluctuations. We run both soaked and unsoaked CBR on remolded specimens compacted to project density targets, so the design accounts for the worst-case saturation scenario that League City’s humid subtropical climate and occasional ponding can create. For projects where deeper bearing layers matter, we often pair the CBR lab work with site-specific compaction control data to verify that what leaves the lab matches what gets built.

Soaked CBR on Gulf Coast clays can drop below 3 percent once saturation reaches field capacity—pavement thickness doubles compared to an unsoaked design assumption.

Process overview

One thing we see repeatedly in League City is that a soil’s visual classification does not predict its CBR value. Two clays with similar plasticity can show a 300 percent difference in soaked CBR just because of silt content and compaction moisture sensitivity. Our lab procedure starts with a standard or modified Proctor to establish the moisture-density relationship, then remolds specimens at the target water content for a three-point CBR curve. We apply the surcharge weight that corresponds to the final pavement section, typically 10 or 15 pounds for flexible pavement. Penetration readings at 0.1 and 0.2 inches are corrected against the standard crushed-stone reference, and we report both the corrected CBR and the swell percentage after a 96-hour soak. For League City projects near tidal channels, where groundwater may be within four feet of finished grade, the soaked CBR often governs the structural number, and we flag any swell exceeding 1.5 percent as a constructability risk that may require lime treatment or undercut.

Local ground factors

In League City, we face two key issues: heavy rainfall exceeding 55 inches per year and high water tables in many areas east of I-45. Designing a pavement base solely with an unsoaked CBR can lead to subgrade saturation during the first monsoon or tropical storm, reducing bearing capacity by 50% or more. The only way to directly measure this strength reduction before paving is through a soaked CBR test in the lab. Additionally, imported fill from borrow pits east of Galveston Bay sometimes contains sulfates. If a chemical analysis isn't done alongside the CBR, sulfate-induced heave can ruin a pavement within three years regardless of the CBR value. We advise conducting water-soluble sulfate tests with every CBR program on imported fill, and any sulfate over 0.3% triggers a stabilization review.

CBR with Chemical Stabilization Screening

For subgrades in League City with a plasticity index greater than 25 or swell above 2%, we test parallel CBR samples mixed with lime or cement at 4%, 6%, and 8% by weight. After a 7-day cure, the soaked CBR provides the design team with a stabilized modulus to optimize pavement thickness and meet TxDOT standards.

Reference standards

We follow these standards: ASTM D1883-21 (CBR test), ASTM D1557-12(2021) (modified Proctor), ASTM D698-12(2021) (standard Proctor), TxDOT Tex-120-E (compaction), Tex-121-E (lab CBR), and AASHTO T 193-22 (CBR).

Quick answers

What is the difference between soaked and unsoaked CBR, and which one should I use for League City pavement design?

An unsoaked CBR tests a sample compacted at field moisture without soaking, while a soaked CBR immerses the sample for 96 hours to mimic long-term groundwater and rain effects. In League City, with over 55 inches of annual rain and shallow water tables, the soaked value usually controls. For flexible or rigid pavement design per AASHTO 93, we recommend using soaked CBR, reserving the unsoaked value only for temporary platforms or dry-season work.

How much does a laboratory CBR test cost for a League City project?

A single-point soaked CBR with Proctor and swell testing typically costs between $120 and $190, depending on whether a one-point, two-point, or three-point curve is needed and the number of samples. A full three-point CBR with additional index tests like Atterberg limits, gradation, and sulfate screening is at the higher end. We provide a per-sample quote after reviewing your boring logs and project details.

How many CBR specimens do I need for a commercial site in League City?

The number of CBR tests depends on soil variability. For a typical commercial pad or parking lot under 5 acres with uniform geology, we suggest one CBR per major soil unit—usually two or three specimens. If the site spans different geomorphic units, like Pleistocene terrace deposits and Holocene floodplain clays, each needs its own test. We use your boring logs to determine the minimum statistically valid testing program.

Can you run CBR on stabilized subgrade or base material?

Absolutely. We frequently test cement-, lime-, and fly-ash-stabilized soils and base courses for League City projects. The method follows ASTM D1883 but with a 7-day moist cure at controlled temperature before the 96-hour soak. We report both the immediate CBR after compaction and the cured, soaked CBR so the designer can assess strength gain from stabilization and adjust the structural number as needed.
